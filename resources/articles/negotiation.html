Delegate Resources · Category G: Negotiation

Multilateral Negotiation & Consensus.

By SKIT MUN Editorial Team · 8 Min Read

Model United Nations is an exercise in coalition building, not unilateral decree. Awards and impactful resolutions are won by delegates who construct inclusive consensus rather than those who bully opposing opinions.

1. The Harvard Principled Negotiation Framework in MUN

Adapted from Roger Fisher and William Ury's seminal negotiations research, successful MUN diplomacy applies four foundational tenets:

  • Separate the People from the Problem: Treat opposing delegates with profound courtesy, even when their national policy conflicts directly with yours. Hostility breeds retaliatory voting.
  • Focus on Underlying Interests, Not Entrenched Positions: A nation demanding strict maritime border inspections might be driven by fear of unregistered drug trafficking. By addressing the trafficking concern through multilateral radar sharing, both nations satisfy their national objectives without compromising sovereignty.
  • Invent Options for Mutual Gain: Avoid binary either/or traps. Propose phased timelines, opt-in transitional clauses, and international monitoring committees.
  • Insist on Objective Criteria: Anchor negotiations in existing UN precedents, verifiable IMF statistical frameworks, and treaty jurisprudence.

2. Understanding Red Lines and Bargaining Chips

Every sovereign state enters committee with non-negotiable boundaries ("Red Lines") and flexible policy areas ("Bargaining Chips"):

  • Red Lines: Core sovereign mandates your nation can never concede (e.g., Israel and self-defense jurisdiction, China and internal regional affairs, the United States and ICC jurisdiction over military personnel).
  • Bargaining Chips: Concessions you can grant to neighboring delegations in exchange for their co-sponsorship on your critical operational clauses (e.g., agreeing to host a regional headquarters in their capital, supporting higher funding allocations for their regional sub-committee).

3. Overcoming Deadlocks in Committee

When two dominant blocs refuse to merge their working papers, an astute delegate acts as the diplomatic bridge:

  • Propose Bilateral Working Group Chits: Send a chit proposing a 3-minute private caucus during the next unmod with the lead sponsor of the rival paper.
  • Drafting Compromise Language: Replace contentious mandatory verbs ("Demands", "Orders") with consensual multilateral phrasing ("Calls upon member states to consider", "Encourages the establishment of voluntary monitoring frameworks").
  • Constructing Annexes & Opt-in Clauses: Place disputed technical protocols in voluntary annexes, allowing broad ratification of the core treaty while giving reluctant member states time to adapt domestic legislation.
