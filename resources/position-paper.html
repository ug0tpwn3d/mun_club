Position Papers.

SKIT MUN Editorial Desk · Standard Policy Guide

An AIPPM Position Paper is a concise policy document that presents your assigned political party's or portfolio's position on the agenda. Unlike a conventional MUN position paper, it does not focus on representing a foreign nation's interests or citing international treaties. Instead, it demonstrates your understanding of Indian politics, governance, legislation, the party's ideology, its historical position on the issue, and the practical reforms it would support. The paper should serve as a clear political and policy roadmap for your interventions throughout committee.

1. Understanding the AIPPM Position Paper

In AIPPM, you represent a political party, political leader, or designated portfolio, depending on the committee structure. Your position paper must therefore remain consistent with the political identity you have been assigned. The objective is not simply to explain whether you personally agree or disagree with the agenda, but to demonstrate what your assigned political entity has historically believed, advocated, opposed, implemented, or proposed.

Your paper should answer three fundamental questions: What is the problem? Where does your party or portfolio stand on the problem? What policy or legislative course of action would your side pursue to address it?

2. Four-Part Standard Architecture

A strong AIPPM position paper should generally be organized into four clear sections. The exact format may differ between conferences, so always check the individual committee guidelines before submission.

  • Section I: Background & Issue Analysis: Introduce the agenda and explain the political, constitutional, social, economic, or administrative dimensions of the issue. Identify the origin of the problem, important developments, affected stakeholders, and why the matter requires political or legislative attention. Avoid turning this section into a generic essay; the background should directly establish the problem that your party is expected to deliberate upon.
  • Section II: Party Ideology & Historical Position: Explain how your assigned political party or portfolio approaches the issue. Refer to the party manifesto, official statements, parliamentary interventions, legislation supported or opposed, government decisions, election promises, committee reports, and other documented positions where relevant. The objective is to establish a credible and research-backed political position rather than inventing a stance for the purpose of committee.
  • Section III: Current Political & Policy Position: Analyse the present situation and clearly establish what your party or portfolio would argue in committee. Address the major disagreements surrounding the agenda, the interests of different stakeholders, constitutional or legal considerations, and the practical limitations of existing policy. Where the assigned portfolio is an individual political figure, distinguish between the individual's documented position and the broader position of their political party.
  • Section IV: Proposed Solutions & Legislative Measures: Present realistic and implementable solutions that are consistent with the position you have established. These may include amendments to existing laws, new legislation, administrative mechanisms, institutional reforms, funding mechanisms, regulatory changes, inter-governmental coordination, or measurable implementation frameworks. Solutions should explain not only what should be done, but also who should implement it, how it can be financed or administered, and how its effectiveness can be evaluated.

3. Research Your Party Before Writing

AIPPM requires a different style of preparation from a conventional country-based committee. Before drafting your position paper, research the political party and portfolio you have been assigned rather than beginning with your own personal opinion on the agenda. Study the party's manifesto, official website, speeches, parliamentary record, relevant legislation, press releases, election promises, major policy announcements, and publicly documented statements by senior leaders.

Also identify disagreements within the political landscape. A party may support the broad objective of a policy while disagreeing with its implementation, funding mechanism, constitutional basis, administrative structure, or timeline. These distinctions are often more valuable in AIPPM than simply stating that a party is "for" or "against" an issue. Strong preparation allows you to defend your position when other delegates challenge its consistency or historical record.

4. Evidence, Legal Framework & Political Record

Every major factual assertion should be supported by credible research. Depending on the agenda, relevant sources can include the Constitution of India, Acts of Parliament, parliamentary debates, government notifications, official committee reports, Supreme Court judgments, Election Commission publications, ministry documents, party manifestos, and officially published statements.

Use political history carefully. A past statement should not automatically be presented as the party's current position unless it remains relevant and there is no documented change in policy. Similarly, criticism made by an opposing party should be identified as an allegation or political position rather than presented as an established fact. Research should allow you to distinguish between a party's official position, the record of a government it participated in, statements made by individual leaders, and your own proposed committee strategy.

5. Writing Style & Representation

Write from the perspective of your assigned political entity, not from your personal perspective. Avoid phrases such as "I believe", "I think", or "in my opinion". Depending on the format of the conference, formulations such as "The party maintains", "The delegation proposes", or "The Honourable Member supports" are generally more appropriate.

Keep the language formal, precise, and politically credible. AIPPM does not reward unnecessarily complicated vocabulary. Your objective is to communicate a clear political argument, establish the reasoning behind it, and support it with evidence. A well-written paper should make the delegate's position understandable even when the reader has not studied the entire agenda in advance.

Avoid excessive rhetoric, personal attacks, unsupported accusations, and dramatic language. Political disagreement is expected in AIPPM, but your position paper should demonstrate policy understanding rather than function as an election speech.

6. Common Pitfalls to Avoid

The most common mistake is writing a generic essay about the agenda instead of a position paper. A description of the issue alone does not establish your party's position. Similarly, copying a party manifesto without connecting it to the committee agenda does not demonstrate adequate preparation.

  • Do not represent India as a country delegation: AIPPM is centred on domestic political positions, parties, portfolios, governance, legislation, and parliamentary debate rather than the foreign-policy framework normally used in country-based MUN committees.
  • Do not invent party positions: Where a party has a documented stance, research and represent it accurately. Where no clear position exists, distinguish between an established position and a reasonable policy proposal for the simulation.
  • Do not ignore your portfolio: If you are representing an individual political leader, your arguments should remain reasonably consistent with that person's documented political position, responsibilities, and public record.
  • Do not rely entirely on rhetoric: Strong speeches cannot compensate for weak research. Be prepared to explain the legal basis, administrative mechanism, financial implications, and expected outcome of major proposals.
  • Do not make unrealistic promises: A proposal should account for constitutional authority, legislative procedure, institutional capacity, funding, federal relations, and practical implementation where applicable.
  • Do not confuse personal opinion with political representation: Your role in AIPPM is to argue the position you have been assigned while demonstrating independent analysis of the issue. Your preparation should therefore focus on accurate political representation, not simply on what you personally would prefer.

7. The Final Test

Before submitting your position paper, read it as though you were another delegate trying to challenge your position. You should be able to identify your party's stance, understand the reasoning behind it, point to the evidence supporting it, identify the weaknesses in the current system, and explain the concrete measures your delegation would support.

Your position paper should ultimately give you a foundation for the entire committee: speeches, moderated caucuses, negotiations, points of disagreement, alliances, legislative proposals, and responses to opposition arguments. In AIPPM, preparation is not about memorising a speech. It is about understanding your political position well enough to defend it when the room begins to challenge it.
